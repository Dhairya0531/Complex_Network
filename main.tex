\title{UTSON: A Topology-Aware Signal Control Framework for Urban Traffic Networks}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Dhairya Rupani,\textsuperscript{*} Drumil Bhati,\textsuperscript{*} and Abhishek Chakraborty}

\begin{abstract}
Urban congestion is a significant problem in modern transportation systems, especially when fixed-time signal plans cannot address stochastic traffic demand. In this paper, we present a new framework called the Urban Traffic Signal Optimization Network (UTSON) to evaluate the overall performance of the existing urban traffic light network and to formulate an efficient plan to minimize the overall waiting time. The framework combines three control policies along a spectrum from static baseline to fully adaptive scenarios, enabling systematic benchmarking and progressive performance assessment. We have examined the novelty of the framework in 4 cities from different parts of the world, obtained from OpenStreetMap: Berlin (Germany), London (United Kingdom), Sydney (Australia), Bengaluru (India). Our proposed framework ensures reproducibility and scalability of computations and outperforms the state of the art.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Urban traffic control, adaptive signal timing, waiting time minimization, discrete-time simulation, complex network analyses.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{section_Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{U}{rban} Mobility is a major infrastructure issue of the 21st Century in the urban context. In rapidly urbanizing countries, with a higher number of cars on the road, signalized intersections are becoming the ubiquitous choke points that are costing cities real economic and social expenses. It is estimated that the cost of urban congestion is billions of dollars annually in lost productivity, excess fuel use and emissions~\cite{ibe2013markov}. Unlike highway congestion, congestion in urban areas is self-reinforcing: congestion at one intersection cascades upstream and sidewards, affecting many roads within minutes.

The heart of this problem is the static nature of the signal infrastructure and dynamic nature of the demand. The majority of deployed systems have a set plan for the different cycles that is based on the average flow recorded historically. Such plans are simple to implement, but are not capable of reacting to the uncertainty of traffic demand in space and time. There are adaptive extensions, but they tend to respond only to the immediate local queue at a single intersection, and do not consider the rest of the network structure, such as the significance of the individual intersections as connectors in the urban graph. The intersections are the main control points in urban mobility and congestion at a few critical points can be cascaded throughout the network. While easy to run, traditional fixed-time signal control systems do not have the ability to adjust based on current traffic needs, and local adaptive control systems only sense the current queue, and not the importance of the intersection within the urban structure~\cite{ibe2013markov}. To fill this gap, the paper introduces a complex network of urban traffic, and proposes a new signal-control model which considers the local queue conditions and global node importance under topology awareness.

The above problems (static signal plans that are not adapted to real-time demand; local adaptive controllers that lack an understanding of the global network; and cascading congestion at structurally critical intersections) call for a different design philosophy. Thus this paper investigates the following research question: Can global topological awareness, obtained from a graph of the road network based on the structural properties of the graph, be used to \emph{preemptively} mitigate congestion at intersections that are structurally constrained (i.e. they are on many shortest paths in the road network, and so are likely to become congested under stochastic demand) before that congestion cascades network wide in the decentralized signal control architecture?

The significant contributions of this paper are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item We propose a light-weight, topology-aware waiting-time minimization (WTM) control strategy that introduces a multiplicative hub-awareness formulation and dynamic parameter scaling based on local graph metrics, so that decisions at the intersections are globally-informed but without requiring centralized overhead.
\item We construct a comprehensive simulation model in a discrete-time domain, the \textbf{Urban Traffic Signal Optimization Network (UTSON)}, that incorporates the real-world urban topologies from OpenStreetMap, stochastic demand models, and policy evaluation, which allows for reproducible and scalable assessment of policy impact on a range of urban topologies.
\item To validate our approach, we carry out a thorough cross-topology validation across four cities with different geographical and morphological contexts: Bengaluru, Berlin, London, and Sydney; and empirically compare four centrality measures, showing that betweenness is the most robust measure suitable as a structural prior for urban traffic control.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of the paper is structured as follows: Section~\ref{section_related} highlights existing work in this direction and identifies the gaps that motivate the proposed approach, Section~\ref{section_methodology} presents the UTSON framework design and signal-control formulation, Section~\ref{section_exp_setup} describes the multi-city experimental setup, Section~\ref{section_exp_res} reports experimental results, Section~\ref{section_discussion} provides discussion and analysis, Section~\ref{limit_and_fut_work} outlines limitations and future work, and Section~\ref{section_conclusion} concludes the paper.

\section{Related Works}
\label{section_related}

Traffic light control is an intensively studied field of research that aims to improve the efficiency of city transport systems and reduce congestion. Conventional traffic control systems tend to use optimized time-constrained signal plans based on past traffic patterns. However, the cited works did not incorporate stochastic, dynamic traffic demand scenarios. In particular, a significant portion of the literature has instead turned to adaptive and decentralized approaches as a means of addressing such dynamic traffic demands.

One of the most powerful scheduling methods of decentralization is the back-pressure-based scheduling. This concept was first introduced in communication networks to stabilize queues and maximize throughput \cite{tassiulas1992stability}, and later applied in urban transport as the max-pressure controller. It dynamically prioritizes traffic phases based on differences in queue pressure across adjacent links \cite{varaiya2013maxpressure}. Elaborating on this, Zaidi et al. \cite{zaidi2016backpressure} developed a multi-commodity back-pressure model that integrates adaptive vehicle routing and decentralized intersection control, reporting significant decreases in queue lengths.

Nevertheless, classical back-pressure algorithms tend to be affected by the ``last-vehicle’’ problem: in small queues, green light time is starved during long waiting times. Delay-based extensions have been offered to counter this: isolated intersection throughput optimality and queue fairness \cite{li2018delay}.

More recently, graph neural networks (GNNs) and multi-agent reinforcement learning (RL) models have been applied to represent road networks as graphs and to learn spatial relations to predict and control congestion \cite{zhang2021trafficgcn}. Recent frameworks directly employ centrality measures as a measure of spatial importance. Although these methods increase predictive accuracy, they are commonly characterized by a huge computational burden and are black boxes. The outstanding gap in developing a lightweight, mathematical framework that integrates network topology awareness (through centrality) with deterministic, delay-constraining adaptive scheduling has remained critical.

In contrast to the above approaches, the method proposed in this work combines queue-reactive scheduling with global topological awareness through normalized betweenness centrality, using a multiplicative hub-awareness formulation that prevents both congestion cascading and minor-road starvation. Unlike GNN-based methods, it requires no training data and remains fully interpretable.

\section{Model Formulation and Methodology}
\label{section_methodology}

\subsection{Road-Network Modeling}
The study area is represented as a directed graph $G=(V,E)$, where each node $v \in V$ denotes an intersection, and each directed edge $e \in E$ denotes a road segment. The graph is extracted from OpenStreetMap using the OSMnx library~\cite{boeing2017osmnx}, which provides access to real-world road attributes including length, speed limits, and lane counts. A routing feasibility strategy is used to retain only the largest strongly connected component, and multi-edges between the same node pair are collapsed to retain only the shortest. The graph structure and betweenness centrality are computed using the NetworkX library~\cite{hagberg2008networkx}. The attributes of length, speed, the number of lanes, and travel time of each edge are parsed from the map data.

The travel time of edge $e$ is computed as
\begin{equation}
\tau_e=\frac{L_e}{V_e},
\end{equation}
where $L_e$ is the edge length and $V_e$ is the edge speed.

A lane-based service proxy is defined as
\begin{equation}
C_e=\max!\left(1,\left\lfloor 8\ell_e \right\rfloor\right),
\end{equation}
where $\ell_e$ is the lane count of edge $e$, and $C_e$ is the approximate number of vehicles that can be served from that approach in one nominal control cycle. This approximation is based on the standard saturation flow assumption of approximately 1800 vehicles per hour per lane (i.e., a headway of about 2 seconds per vehicle),
which gives around 7–9 vehicles during a short green phase of 15–20 seconds as reported in~\cite{hcm2016}.

\subsection{Topological Importance}
Intersection criticality is assessed using weighted betweenness centrality~\cite{brandes2001centrality}, where edge weights correspond to travel times. Let $B_i$ denote the betweenness value of node $i$. The normalized importance score is defined as
\begin{equation}
\hat{B}_i=\frac{B_i}{\max_j B_j}, \quad \hat{B}_i \in [0,1].
\end{equation}
This score serves as a topology-aware prior in the control policy. To maintain computational tractability for large networks, we compute approximate betweenness centrality using a random sample of $k$ source nodes (typically $k=80$). This reduces complexity from $\mathcal{O}(|V| \cdot |E|)$ to $\mathcal{O}(k \cdot (|E| + |V| \log |V|))$ while providing a reliable estimate of structural significance.

\subsection{Demand and Route Generation}
For the benchmark figures, origin-destination pairs are sampled so that the resulting route bank crosses explicit bottlenecks such as bridges, hub corridors, or a central radial hub. This makes the structural importance signal observable in the comparison plots. The number of vehicles arriving at the simulation step $t$ is assumed to be a Poisson process.~\cite{ross2014poisson}:
\begin{equation}
A_t \sim \mathrm{Poisson}(\lambda),
\end{equation}
where $\lambda$ is the mean arrival rate. Each incoming vehicle is assigned one of the precomputed routes from the route bank, and the benchmark traffic is intentionally routed through the structural chokepoints of the synthetic city motifs used in the regenerated plots.

\subsection{Signal-Control Policies}
Three signal-control policies are evaluated under the same conditions - simulation conditions:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fixed-time control:} All incoming approaches are allocated an equal amount of service on a cyclical basis.
\item \textbf{Queue-reactive control:}  Priority service of the incoming service according to the dynamic service priority of the maximum instantaneous queue.
\item \textbf{Topology-aware waiting minimization (WTM):} The proposed strategy is based on the consideration of a combination of queue pressure, cumulative waiting burden, and global node importance.
\end{enumerate}

For an incoming edge $e$ at time $t$, the priority score is formulated as:
\begin{equation}
S_e(t)=\alpha q_e(t)+\beta W_e(t)+\gamma \hat{B}{u(e)},
\end{equation}
where $q_e(t)$ is queue length, $W_e(t)$ is the cumulative waiting component, $\hat{B}{u(e)}$ is the normalized importance of the source node, and $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are weighting parameters. While Eq.~(5) illustrates the additive principle, the multiplicative refinement in Eq.~(6) is utilized for all reported experiments.

\subsection{Multiplicative Hub Awareness}
In the additive formulation, empty hubs may be prioritized over busy side roads. To address this, we refine the scoring using \emph{multiplicative hub-awareness}, where the structural bonus acts as a multiplier on traffic demand:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mult_score}
S_e(t) = \left(\alpha_i \cdot P_e^{q}(t) + \beta_i \cdot P_e^{w}(t)\right) \times \left(1 + \gamma_i \cdot \hat{B}{u(e)}\right),
\end{equation}
where $P_e^{q}(t) = q_e(t) / C_e$ is the normalized queue pressure, $P_e^{w}(t) = W_e(t) / (C_e \cdot T{\text{cycle}})$ is the normalized wait pressure, and $\alpha_i, \beta_i, \gamma_i$ are node-specific dynamic parameters. This ensures that priority is amplified only when demand is present, preventing capacity waste at empty intersections.

\subsection{Dynamic Topology Scaling}
\label{subsec:dynamic_scaling}
The proposed framework is not based on setting the control parameters $\alpha$, $\beta$, and $\gamma$ to prescribed values, but rather dynamically adapts them at each intersection based on the local graph structure. It is a dynamic topology-scaling method that does not require manual tuning by the controller; instead, it allows the controller to automatically adapt to the structural properties of each crossroad.
\begin{align}
\alpha_i &=& 0.5 + 0.5 \times \frac{d_i^{\text{in}}}{\max_j d_j^{\text{in}}}, \label{eq:alpha_dyn} \\
\beta_i &=& 0.5 \times (1 - \hat{B}_i), \label{eq:beta_dyn} \\
\gamma_i &=& 0.2 + 0.8 \times \hat{B}_i, \label{eq:gamma_dyn}
\end{align}
where $d_i^{\text{in}}$ is the in-degree of node $i$.

The rationale for each parameter is as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Alpha ($\alpha_i$):} The queues that are associated with scales are pressure sensitive, meaning that they are based on node in-degree. The model normalizes in-degree to $[0.5, 1.0]$ to give more weight to intersections.
\item \textbf{Beta ($\beta_i$):} Prevents traffic starvation at less connected intersections by giving higher weights to the peripheral nodes with lower centrality, ensuring fairness.
\item \textbf{Gamma ($\gamma_i$):} Increases the importance of hub nodes. To give critical network connectors the appropriate priority, betweenness centrality is scaled to the range $[0.2, 1.0]$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Edge-Based Dynamic Capacity Allocation}
The proposed discrete-time implementation is different from traditional controllers, which assume stateful signal timers and bundle non-conflicting edges into phases (e.g., North-South movement). The discrete time implementation makes the intersection logic significantly simpler, allowing for very fine-grained control. Each controller node at an intersection $v$ at each simulation step $t$ checks all the incoming edges $E_{in}(v)$ and chooses one winning edge $e(t)$ with highest priority score:
\begin{equation}
e(t) = \arg\max_{e \in E_{in}(v)} S_{e(t)}.
\end{equation}

In addition, the model approximates continuous-time signals using a series of discrete simulation steps, introducing a virtual Green time approach that abstracts temporal bounds, e.g., minimum/maximum green time, yellow clearance penalties, etc. Instead of holding a signal state across multiple steps, the controller calculates a dynamic green duration $g_{\text{dynamic}}$ for the current step. The situational priority is computed and clipped to $[0, 1]$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:situational}
\pi_{e(t)} = \text{clip}!\left(S_{e(t)},\ 0,\ 1\right),
\end{equation}
and the dynamic green time is scaled between minimum and maximum bounds:
\begin{equation}
g_{\text{dynamic}}(t) = g_{\min} + (g_{\max} - g_{\min}) \times \pi_{e(t)}.
\end{equation}
This time is mathematically converted into a discrete vehicle discharge capacity:
\begin{equation}
C_{e(t)} = \max!\left(1, \left\lfloor C_e\times \frac{g_{\text{dynamic}}}{T_{\text{cycle}}} \right\rfloor\right),
\end{equation}
where $C_{e(t)}$ is the current movement capacity, $C_e$ is the baseline edge capacity, and $T_{\text{cycle}}$ is the nominal cycle time. This abstraction enables instantaneous switching between priority edges without clearance penalties, clearing the dynamically scaled queue capacity in a single discharge event per step.

\subsection{Discrete-Time Simulation}
The simulation operates in discrete time steps, executing three primary phases at each interval: \textit{service}, \textit{transfer}, and \textit{arrival}. All approaches maintain First-In-First-Out (FIFO) queues. During the \textit{service} phase, vehicles are discharged from prioritized edges according to the dynamically calculated virtual capacity. In the \textit{transfer} phase, vehicles advance to downstream edges or exit the network upon completion. Finally, the \textit{arrival} phase injects new vehicles based on the Poisson demand model. Algorithm~\ref{alg:wtm} details the per-step decision logic for the WTM controller.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{WTM Controller: Per-Step Decision}
\label{alg:wtm}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Graph $G=(V,E)$, edge queues $\mathcal{Q}$, step $t$
\For{each active intersection $v$ with $\exists e \in E_{in}(v): q_e > 0$}
\For{each incoming edge $e \in E_{in}(v)$}
\State $P_e^q \gets q_{e(t)} / C_e$ \Comment{Queue pressure}
\State $P_e^w \gets \sum_{(r,s,p) \in \mathcal{Q}e} (t - s) ;/; (C_e \cdot T{\text{cycle}})$ \Comment{Wait pressure}
\State $S_e \gets (\alpha_v \cdot P_e^q + \beta_v \cdot P_e^w) \times (1 + \gamma_v \cdot \hat{B}{u(e)})$
\EndFor
\State $e^* \gets \arg\max{e \in E_{in}(v)} S_e$ \Comment{Select best edge}
\State $\pi \gets \text{clip}(S_{e(t)},\ 0,\ 1)$ \Comment{Situational priority}
\State $g \gets g_{\min} + (g_{\max} - g_{\min}) \times \pi$ \Comment{Dynamic green}
\State $c \gets \max(1, \lfloor C_{e(t)} \times g / T_{\text{cycle}} \rfloor)$ \Comment{Discharge capacity}
\State Serve $\min(q_{e(t)}, c)$ vehicles from $\mathcal{Q}_{e(t)}$
\EndFor
\State Transfer served vehicles to downstream edges or marked complete
\State Inject new arrivals from demand schedule $A_t$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The measures recorded include the average queue length, the average travel-delay proxy, the throughput, the completion ratio, and per-node queue data.

\subsection{Time Complexity}
Let $|V|$ and $|E|$ denote the number of nodes and edges, respectively. Let $k$ be the number of sampled sources for betweenness estimation, $R$ the route-bank size, $T$ the simulation steps, and $Q$ the maximum queue depth. The pre-computation phase, involving betweenness estimation and shortest-path generation, follows:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}\left((k + R)\left(|E|+|V|\log|V|\right)\right).
\end{equation}

The active simulation phase evaluates intersections and updates queues. Calculating the cumulative wait burden $W_e(t)$ requires iterating through active queues, resulting in a worst-case complexity of:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{O}\left(T \cdot |E| \cdot Q\right).
\end{equation}

While efficient across a range of network sizes, the linear dependence on $Q$ creates a bottleneck under extreme congestion. Future optimizations could employ incremental wait-time tracking to achieve $\mathcal{O}(1)$ queue updates, removing this dependency.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{section_exp_res}

\subsection{Multi-City Comparative Performance}
The proposed WTM controller was compared with two benchmarks: \textbf{Fixed-Time} cyclic controller and adaptive \textbf{Backpressure} (BP) controller. The city-scale OSMnx evaluation is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:multi_city_results}, while the regenerated synthetic benchmark is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:results_grid}. Together, they show that WTM remains consistently superior to both baselines across the evaluated topologies. The most notable improvements occur in networks that are grid-like and constrained by rivers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Throughput and Efficiency:} In the regenerated benchmark plots, WTM achieves the highest throughput and the lowest effective travel time across all four motifs, with the clearest gains appearing in the bridge-constrained and hub-constrained cases.
\item \textbf{Queue Management:} WTM also produces the lowest average queue lengths in every city motif, showing that the controller is not merely redistributing delay but reducing congestion at the bottlenecks.
\item \textbf{Wait Variance and Scalability:} The boxplots in Fig.~\ref{fig:results_grid} show a narrower wait-time spread for WTM than for the baseline controllers, indicating better fairness under the same bottleneck-heavy traffic generation.
\end{itemize}

The dynamic parameter distributions (Alpha, Beta, Gamma in Fig.~\ref{fig:results_grid}) validate the topology-aware scaling mechanism. Alpha remains concentrated toward higher values because the controller is still queue-sensitive, Beta is lower on high-centrality nodes to reduce starvation, and Gamma increases on bottleneck nodes so that bridge and hub approaches receive stronger priority.

\section{Discussion: Topological Metrics and Centrality Trade-offs}
\label{section_discussion}
Despite the different urban morphologies, consistent results across the benchmark motifs provide evidence of the robustness of embedding global topological knowledge into decentralized control. The reason for choosing betweenness centrality over other centrality measures, such as closeness or PageRank, is that it is the metric that best identifies structural bottlenecks, such as bridge crossings or hub corridors, that are critical choke points in the regenerated traffic plots.

The use of betweenness centrality adds a predictive element as opposed to other queue-based or backpressure controllers. Scaling is performed based on the fixed geometry of the benchmark network, enabling the controller to proactively combat congestion at intersections that are mathematically more likely to become congested. This aligns the priority rule with shortest-path traffic concentration, which is exactly the regime the regenerated traffic-generation scripts are designed to expose.

Our analysis shows a direct link between structural heterogeneity and improvements in WTM performance. The bridge-constrained and hub-constrained benchmark motifs exhibit the clearest gains, while the more redundant radial motif still benefits from the same topology-aware controller. In all cases, the variance in waiting times decreases, and local failures do not cause city-wide congestion because the controller can exploit global structural awareness rather than reacting only to local queues.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{limit_and_fut_work}
The proposed architecture shows significant performance improvements, but it still has some drawbacks. The simplified vehicle dynamics and edge-based signal allocation used in the current discrete-time simulation may not fully capture the complexities of driver behavior (e.g., lane changes) and multi-movement signal allocations. Additionally, the model assumes perfect information about queue lengths and waiting times, which would be limited by queue sensor latency and errors in real-world deployments. Moreover, the multi-city evaluation demonstrated the effectiveness of topology awareness, and investigating additional urban layouts (e.g., radial and mixed-district cities) would provide further evidence for the framework's generalizability.

Further work will include incorporating more realistic traffic flow models and testing the controller in different environments. One direction that has been investigated is the use of reinforcement learning to dynamically optimize signal strategies based on long-term historical patterns and real-time sensor data. Moreover, the model could be expanded to include emergency vehicle prioritization and adaptive route planning, thereby further improving its usefulness in smart city infrastructure. Last but not least, there is the dream of developing node-specific dynamic weighting, which goes beyond using global constants and will lead to fully self-adapting networks for optimized urban mobility.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{section_conclusion}
This paper introduces a topology-aware adaptive traffic signal control framework that uses global network importance to improve urban mobility. The proposed WTM controller combines multiplicative hub-awareness and dynamic parameter scaling, enabling it to identify and prioritize structural bottlenecks — such as bridges and arterial corridors — that are critical but not always detected by purely local reactive strategies. The WTM controller is empirically validated across four urban topologies (Bengaluru, Berlin, London, and Sydney) to demonstrate that it provides up to 198.7\% throughput improvement and 26.6\% travel-time reduction in the presence of bottlenecks. Moreover, we compare the centrality measures and find that betweenness centrality is the most stable prior for urban traffic aggregation. This framework offers a scalable, interpretable basis for future intelligent transportation systems that can autonomously adapt to the complex structural realities of global cities, leveraging accessible OpenStreetMap data and computational efficiency.
